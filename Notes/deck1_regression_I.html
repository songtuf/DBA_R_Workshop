When to use multiple regression

  • Outcome (Y): one continuous variable (sales, satisfaction, spend)
  • Predictors (X): any mix of continuous and categorical
  • Question: the association between each X and Y, adjusting for the others
  • Not (yet) a causal claim. We come back to this tomorrow

The family map: one model, many outfits

This map returns at the start of every topic, with the current method highlighted. PCA/EFA sit outside on purpose: no outcome variable, so they leave the regression family. Saying so gives “everything is regression” an honest boundary.

The model

\[y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ki} + \varepsilon_i\]

  • One equation that holds for each observation \(i = 1, \dots, n\) (each store)
  • \(\beta_j\): expected change in \(y\) for a one-unit change in \(x_j\), holding the other \(x\)s constant
  • \(\varepsilon_i\): everything about store \(i\) the \(x\)s don’t capture

How the line is chosen: OLS

  • Ordinary Least Squares: choose the intercept and slopes that make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible
  • Residual = actual − predicted: what the model missed

No normal equations, no proof. In Block 2 we name why OLS is “best” (BLUE) and under which conditions.

Notation: the textbook

Symbol Meaning
\(n\) sample size (number of observations)
\(k\) number of predictors
\(i\) observation index (store \(i\))
\(\alpha\) intercept
\(\beta_j\) population coefficient for predictor \(j\): unknown
\(\hat\beta_j\) estimate of \(\beta_j\) from our sample
\(\varepsilon_i\) error: unobservable
\(\hat y_i\) fitted (predicted) value for observation \(i\)
\(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\) residual: computable

Fitted model (no error term; it’s a prediction):

\[\hat y_i = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta_1 x_{1i} + \cdots + \hat\beta_k x_{ki}\]

The hat is the core idea: no hat = the truth we want; hat = what our data give us. ε vs. e matters in Block 2: assumptions are about ε, but we can only check them using e, so diagnostics are always indirect. Mention β₀ as the alternative intercept notation verbally.

Notation you’ll see in papers

Recognize these; you don’t need to use them:

  • Matrix form: \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\), the same model written compactly
  • \(b\) vs. \(\hat\beta\): both mean “the estimated coefficient”
  • ⚠️ In many APA-style tables, “β” means the standardized coefficient and “b” or “B” the unstandardized one. This clashes with the textbook use of β as the population value. Always check the table note.
  • \(y_{it}\): observation \(i\) at time \(t\) (panel data); \(y_{ij}\): person \(i\) in firm \(j\)

Block 2 — The Model’s Promises

Why assumptions, before any output?

  • Soon we’ll read standard errors, t-values, p-values, and confidence intervals
  • Every one of those numbers is valid only if the model’s promises hold
  • So first: what does the model promise, about each of its parts?
  • Today: understand the promises. Tomorrow: check them in R

The equation has three parts

\[y_i = \underbrace{\alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ki}}_{\text{systematic part}} \;+\; \underbrace{\varepsilon_i}_{\text{error}}\]

Part of the equation Promise
Systematic part 1. Linearity / correct specification
The \(x\)’s 2. No perfect collinearity
The error \(\varepsilon_i\) 3. Exogeneity · 4. Homoscedasticity · 5. Independence
(optional extra) Normality of the errors: outside Gauss-Markov

Promise 1: Linearity / correct specification

The promise (systematic part): \(y\) really is \(\alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k\) on average: the right form and the right variables.

  • Plain English: a straight-line relationship for each \(x\) (holding the others constant), and nothing important left out or wrongly put in
  • StoreMax example: ad spend may have diminishing returns: the first $5k of ads helps far more than the next $5k. A straight line would misdescribe that.
  • If violated: biased coefficients. The story itself is wrong.
  • Tomorrow: residual plots, component-plus-residual plots; remedies such as logs

Promise 2: No perfect collinearity

The promise (the \(x\)’s): no predictor is an exact linear combination of the others.

  • Plain English: each \(x\) must carry some information the others don’t
  • StoreMax example: including store size in sq ft and sq meters. They are the same variable, so R can’t separate them (it drops one: NA in the output).
  • Near-collinearity is the practical problem: ad spend and store size move together, so their separate effects are hard to pin down
  • If violated: perfect → can’t estimate; near → unstable estimates, inflated SEs
  • Tomorrow: variance inflation factors (VIF)

Promise 3: Exogeneity

The promise (the error): \(E[\varepsilon_i \mid X] = 0\). The error is unrelated to the \(x\)’s.

  • Plain English: whatever we left out of the model is not systematically tied to the predictors we included
  • StoreMax examples:
    • Omitted variable: local competition lowers both prices and sales, and it’s not in the model
    • Reverse causality: head office sends more ad budget to struggling stores, so low sales cause high ad spend, not the other way round
  • If violated: biased coefficients (the endogeneity problem)
  • Tomorrow: this one is not testable from residuals. It is argued from theory and research design.

This is the assumption reviewers ask about most (“what about endogeneity?”). Keep market_affluence out of the examples: it’s tomorrow’s Simpson’s-paradox surprise.

Promise 4: Homoscedasticity

The promise (the error): \(Var(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\), the same for every observation.

  • Plain English: the model’s misses are about equally large everywhere
  • StoreMax example: big stores’ weekly sales swing much more than small stores’. The spread of the errors grows with store size.
  • If violated: coefficients are fine, but standard errors are wrong, so p-values and CIs can’t be trusted
  • Tomorrow: scale-location plot, Breusch-Pagan test; remedy: robust standard errors

Promise 5: Independence

The promise (the error): \(\varepsilon_i\) is unrelated to \(\varepsilon_j\) for different observations.

  • Plain English: one observation’s miss tells us nothing about another’s
  • StoreMax examples:
    • Stores in the same city share local shocks (a storm, a festival)
    • The same store measured every week is not 52 independent observations
  • If violated: coefficients are fine, but standard errors are too small: false confidence
  • Tomorrow: mostly a design question: how was the data collected? Remedy: clustered standard errors

And normality?

Not a Gauss-Markov assumption. An optional extra: \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\).

  • Matters only for exact inference in small samples
  • With reasonable \(n\), the central limit theorem does the heavy lifting
  • Common misconception: “the data must be normal.” No: it’s the errors, and even then only sort of
  • Tomorrow: Q-Q plots (preferred over formal normality tests)

The payoff: Gauss-Markov

If Promises 1–5 hold, OLS is the

Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE)

  • Unbiased: on average, \(\hat\beta_j\) hits the true \(\beta_j\)
  • Best: no other linear unbiased estimator has smaller variance
  • No proof needed: this is the reason OLS is the default

Two kinds of failure

Violations that bias coefficients Violations that corrupt standard errors
Misspecification (1), endogeneity (3) Heteroscedasticity (4), dependence (5)
Your story is wrong Your story may be right, but your certainty is overstated
Hard to fix: needs theory and design Often patchable (robust / clustered SEs)

The first column is fatal; the second is fixable.

Collinearity (2) sits apart: it doesn’t bias anything, it just makes estimates imprecise. Mention it verbally.

Promises are about ε; we only see e

  • The promises concern the errors \(\varepsilon_i\), which we never observe
  • We check them indirectly, using the residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\)
  • Some can’t be checked from residuals at all: exogeneity is argued, not tested
  • Keep the five promises in mind as we read the output next: every SE and p-value we see leans on them

Block 3 — Reading the Output

Notation: R’s formula

The model

\[y_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \varepsilon_i\]

is written in R as

lm(y ~ x1 + x2, data = mydata)
In R Meaning
~ “is modeled by”: outcome on the left, predictors on the right
+ add a predictor
(nothing) the intercept \(\alpha\) is included automatically
(nothing) the error \(\varepsilon_i\) is never written
factor(x) treat x as categorical (creates dummies)
I(x^2) do arithmetic inside the formula

Anatomy: the coefficient table

Column What it is What it is not
Estimate \(\hat\beta_j\), the fitted coefficient a causal effect

Mis-interpretation check ❌

Would a reviewer let these through?

  1. “Ad spend drives sales up by 98 units.”
  2. “The model explains 44% of sales.”
  3. “Price is not significant, so price has no effect.”
  4. “Northeast has the largest effect because its coefficient is biggest.”
  5. “p < .001, so the effect is large.”
  1. causal verb from observational data (and Promise 3 is unverified);
  2. variance, not level; (3) absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence;
  3. dummies are comparisons against the reference, not “effects” ranked by size; (5) significance ≠ magnitude. This game recurs all course, and quiz questions use the same format.

Block 4 — Holding Constant, Partial Correlations, Standardization

What does “holding constant” actually mean?

A coefficient in multiple regression is the slope of Y on X after removing from both what the other predictors explain:

# Step 1: strip from sales what the other Xs explain
r_y <- residuals(lm(sales ~ price + store_size + region, data = storemax))
# Step 2: strip from ad_spend what the other Xs explain
r_x <- residuals(lm(ad_spend ~ price + store_size + region, data = storemax))
# Step 3: regress leftover on leftover
c(residual_slope = unname(coef(lm(r_y ~ r_x))[2]),
  multiple_reg   = unname(coef(m2)["ad_spend"]))
residual_slope   multiple_reg 
      97.80904       97.80904

Same number. That is what “holding constant” means.

Seeing it: the added-variable plot

ggplot(tibble(r_x, r_y), aes(r_x, r_y)) +
  geom_point(alpha = .3) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE) +
  labs(x = "Ad spend, net of other predictors",
       y = "Sales, net of other predictors")

Partial vs. semi-partial correlation

Remove other Xs from… Answers
Partial r both X and Y “How strongly are X and Y related, net of everything else?”
Semi-partial (part) r X only “How much does X uniquely add to explaining Y?” 
partial_r     <- cor(r_y, r_x)
semipartial_r <- cor(storemax$sales, r_x)
c(partial = partial_r, semipartial = semipartial_r)
    partial semipartial 
  0.2968163   0.2328994

Which predictor matters most? The units problem

  • ad_spend is in $000s, price in dollars, store_size in 1,000 sq ft
  • Raw coefficients cannot be compared across predictors: a “unit” means different things
  • Common fix in the literature: standardize (convert to z-scores: mean 0, SD 1)

Three ways to scale a regression

Version Coefficient means…
Raw X, raw Y change in Y units per one-unit change in X
Standardized X only change in Y units per one-SD change in X
Standardized X and Y (“beta weights”) change in SDs of Y per one-SD change in X
  • In simple regression, the fully standardized slope equals r
  • “X only” is often the most readable for business audiences: Y stays in real units

Point to the statistic_ columns: slope t-values are identical across all three. R² identical. Only the estimates (units) and the intercept change.

Output → Sentence: three versions, one finding

  • Raw: “Each additional $1,000 in ad spend is associated with 98 more units per week.”
  • X standardized: “A one-SD increase in ad spend is associated with 359 more units per week.”
  • X & Y standardized: “A one-SD increase in ad spend is associated with a 0.29-SD increase in weekly sales.”

All three, holding price, store size, and region constant.

⚠️ Standardization: read before you interpret

  • Same evidence, new units. Rescaling changes how coefficients read, not what the data say: slope t-values, p-values, and R² are identical. (The intercept’s test changes because its meaning changes.)
  • The intercept changes meaning. With centered X, it’s the predicted Y at the average X. With X and Y both standardized, it’s zero (for continuous predictors).
  • Never standardize a dummy variable. “A one-SD change in being in the Northeast” is meaningless. Interpret dummies in 0/1 form even when everything else is standardized.
  • Be careful with “relative importance.” Comparing beta weights within a model is shaky when predictors are correlated. Comparing them across samples or models is invalid.
  • Check the table note. In many published tables, “β” = standardized, “b”/“B” = unstandardized.
